Party Division

Skip to main content
This table presents party affiliation data based on the traditional two-party structure of the United States, with third-party affiliations noted in the Other column. Statistics listed in this table reflect party division at the start of each Congress (unless otherwise noted) and throughout each Congress (in the drop-down feature) as the ratios changed due to deaths, resignations, filled vacancies, contested elections, or new states entering the Union. The dates provided here are seating dates (unless otherwise noted), with the member’s election or appointment date noted in a footnote. Vacancies of one month or less are not specifically noted. Changes in Senate membership are included when they affected party balance. Third-party senators typically choose to "caucus" or organize with one of the two major parties. When their choice affected the party division, it is noted in a footnote.

Sources for party determination include the Biographical Directory of the U.S. Congress, Kenneth Martis’s Historical Atlas of Political Parties in the United States Congress (1989), and Congressional Quarterly’s Guide to U.S. Elections (2016). The fluidity of the American party system from the nation’s founding through the 19th century makes determining the affiliation of some members at a given moment in time a challenge. While their party affiliations may have changed during their tenure, this table reflects the overall balance of power between the major parties or factions during each Congress.

1. Organized political parties developed in the U.S. in the 1790s, but political factions—from which organized parties evolved—began to appear almost immediately after establishment of the federal government. Those who supported the Washington administration were referred to as "pro-administration" and would eventually form the Federalist Party, while those in opposition joined the emerging (Jeffersonian) Democratic Republican Party.
2. Although 13 Pro-Administration senators and 7 Anti-Administration senators had been elected to the Senate by the time the first Congress convened on March 7, 1789, when quorum was first reached on April 6, 1789, only 7 Pro-Admin and 5 Anti-Admin senators had appeared. Members slowly arrived over the next several months, and by June 27, 1789, when New York's two senators had been sworn in, the balance stood at 15 Pro-Administration and 7 Anti-Administration, with North Carolina and Rhode Island not yet represented in Congress.
3. By June 25, 1790, both North Carolina's and Rhode Island's senators had been sworn in, and John Walker (Pro-Admin) of Virginia, appointed March 31, 1790, had replaced William Grayson (Pro-Admin), who died on March 12, 1790.
4. James Monroe (Anti-Admin) of Virginia, elected November 9, 1790, was seated at the start of the 3rd session, replacing appointed Senator John Walker (Pro-Admin).

1. The Senate stood evenly divided at the opening of the 47th Congress. (Independent David Davis of Illinois caucused with the Democrats; Readjustor William Mahone of Virginia agreed to caucus with Republicans.) Republican Vice President Chester Arthur's tie-breaking vote gave the majority to the Republicans, who maintained a one-seat majority on the committees. But because several Republican members were absent due to illness, Democrats were able to stall the election of new officers. After New York's two Republican senators dramatically resigned on May 16, 1881, the Senate adjourned. On September 19, 1881, President Garfield died (he had been shot on July 2, 1881), and Chester Arthur became president, leaving the vice presidency vacant. Arthur called for a special session to begin on October 10, 1881. With a temporary majority, Democrats insisted on electing a president pro tempore before swearing in the new Republican senators. The two parties then compromised, leaving Republicans in charge of committees and allowing Democrats to elect the Senate's officers and fill patronage positions.
